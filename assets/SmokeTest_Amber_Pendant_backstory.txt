The Lustral Pendant hangs at the throats of those who tend the sacred waters of the Pearl, its ceramic shell catching the humid light of the biodome in fleeting iridescent bands. Each one is shaped by hand from kiln-fired ash harvested from the fungus farms below, pressed into a flattened teardrop the size of a thumb and glazed with the same living biofilm that lines the EZ-aqua basins. At its heart, cradled in a setting of brushed ceramic, rests a single droplet of stabilized amber gel drawn from the Pearl's central reservoir — not ordinary EZ-water, but a slow-tinctured concentrate that has soaked for decades in mineral salts and warm light until it has taken on a color like fossilized honey. When a caretaker wears the pendant against her skin, the warmth of her pulse keeps the gel soft and faintly luminous; in the cool air of the corridors it dims to the color of old tea. The piece is said to hold a quiet affinity with the water it was drawn from, and old women in the hydroponics terraces will tell you that a pendant left too long in darkness begins to weep — a single bead of condensate appearing on its surface as if the water inside is restless.

Only the senior tenders and the revered mothers of the Pearl are entrusted with one. The tradition began in the third generation of the voyage, when a senior cultivator named Breta Sallow asked her apprentices to fashion keepsakes from the residue of her work, so that some part of her attention might remain in the gardens after her hands had grown too stiff to tend them. The pendants were never sold, only gifted, and only to those who had given at least fifteen years of service to the Pearl's living systems. Recipients are expected to wear the piece whenever they enter the canopy or descend to the fungal cellars, a small declaration that they belong to the water and the water belongs to them.

It is Breta Sallow herself, now ninety-one and keeper of the upper terraces, who presses the Lustral Pendant into the palm of her successor on the morning of the change of seasons. Her hands, when she writes in her logbook beneath the inverted canopy, are still steady enough to guide a stylus across the page.

LOGBOOK ENTRY — Breta Sallow, Senior Tender, Pearl Canopy, Third Generation Terrace, Year 891 After Launch

The amber in my pendant caught the dawn light this morning and for a moment I thought it was lit from inside. Foolish, perhaps, to notice such things at my age. I have given away six of these in my life and worn one for sixty-three years, and still the little stone surprises me. Today I will hand mine to Yenna, and tomorrow I will ask her to take the upper beds and the songbirds' roost and all the slow patient work that I have loved. I am not afraid. The water remembers every hand that has cared for it, and I will be in its memory long after the pendant around my neck rests against someone else's throat. The Pearl is kind to its old women. It lets them stay until they are ready to go.
