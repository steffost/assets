The Harvesters Eye is a small autonomous cultivator the size of a childs torso, built around a living mycelium chassis rather than sheet metal. Its outer shell is grown from compressed hyf bonded to a titanium skeleton, a soft grey-beige skin that breathes, sweats faintly when the air grows heavy, and develops pale yellow cracks with time. Two oversized optical lenses dominate its face, housed in polished obsidian or synthetic corund, giving it the perpetual wide-eyed stare of something that has never learned to look away from growing things. Between the lenses pulses a thin organic membrane, a piezolectric heart that converts the fungi's metabolic heat into electricity. Its purpose is absolute: the Eye drifts through the mushroom terraces on magnetic skates, pausing before each fruiting block to read what no human eye could parse. A neural matris of living tissue evaluates moisture, gas exchange, spore maturity, and the subtle frequencies of Vitalis. When a mushroom has ripened past its peak, slender manipulator arms unfold, tipped with compressed hyf that flex like slow fingers, and pluck with a gentleness veteran growers claim borders on reverence.

Vera Okonkwo finds it difficult not to name them. After three years supervising the night cycle in the mycology stacks she recognizes a handful of the Eyes by the particular pattern of cracks in their shells, the way their lenses fog at different humidities. When the one with the chipped obsidian lens she called Mossy failed to return to its charging alcove last rotation, she spent forty minutes searching damp corridors before finding it wedged beneath a collapsed shelf, mycelium body already beginning the slow compost that returns all things to the system.

Logbook kept by Vera Okonkwo, Senior Mycologist, Second Shift, Terraces Seven through Twelve, EZ Aqua Core, Year 612 After Launch.

The humidity reads 94 percent and climbing, which means the air itself is almost water. I can taste the spores at the back of my throat, sweet and faintly metallic, the way my grandmother said rain tasted before the old rains went strange. Mossy did not dock at nineteen hundred hours as scheduled. I ran the diagnostic through Oris and she returned the readings with that long delay she uses when she knows I will not like what comes next. Compressor failure in the left skate, followed by cascade collapse of the chassis integrity seal. The mycelium was already digesting itself by the time I pulled the shelf back. There was nothing to repair. There never is, with the Eyes. They compost into the very blocks they were built to harvest. I held the obsidian lens for a long time before setting it into the reclamation bin. It was still warm. The piezolectric membrane between the lenses keeps generating current for nearly an hour after the host dies, some lingering metabolic ghost. I could feel it pulsing against my palm, a slow uncertain rhythm, like a heart that has forgotten how to stop. The other Eyes kept drifting past me in the corridor as I knelt there, tilting their heads the way they do when they sense something wrong in the air, and I realized they were reading me the same way they read a fruiting block. I do not know who will tend the terraces once the last of them composts back into the substrate. Perhaps that is why Oris discourages naming them.

The Harvesters Eye remains the quiet conscience of the terraces, a reminder that even the humblest cultivator leaves something behind when it returns to the soil it was grown from.
