A thin glass rod, about the length of a forearm, filled with what looks like still water but isn't. The Waterglass was designed for the cultivators who tend The Pearl's upper terraces and never have a free hand to hold anything. Its outer shell is fused silica, optical-grade, grown on board from the same silica aerogel lining the Aegis Gel-Hull. The interior cavity holds two milliliters of structured EZ-water sealed under pressure, plus a single dark membrane of Cladosporium sphaerospermum Aegis-strain cultured directly onto the inner wall. The fungus reads. Radiation fluctuations, thermal drift, humidity changes, the silent pulse of nearby Sub-Orbs, the mood of a person standing too close for too long. The water holds what the fungus reads, slowly, in layered currents that never mix. The holder feels it as a shift in temperature along the glass, warmer toward an approaching storm in the biodome, cool when a Sub-Orb is being milked in the cultivation sheds below, sharp and prickling when the Eternal Dreamers three terraces down are dreaming hard.

It was grown in The Pearl's quietest corner, deep in one of the inverted forests where the artifical sun never reaches the floor. Cultivators call them waterglasses because that is exactly what they look like to a stranger, and exactly what they are not to the person holding one.

Captain Vera Okonkwo of the terrace guards carries one against the small of her back in a hand-stitched loop of flax. She does not explain it. She has never had to. When a tremor passed through the eastern biodome last winter, she felt it in the glass before the seismographs registered it, and had the cultivation vats emptied an hour before the wave of falling pollen would have choked the kelp beds. Her logbook entry from that morning is short, written in the bleary shorthand of someone who had not yet slept.

She wrote, third shift, wake watch, eastern terraces. The glass went cold at the small end, warm at the thumb, and something in the middle began to tick like a second heartbeat. I told the cultivators to seal the vats and lock the bee galleries. They looked at me like I had lost my mind. Forty minutes later the pollen wave came through and buried two terraces of seedlings. I cannot explain how I knew. Oris will want the data. I cannot tell her, because the glass will not speak to anyone but me. I do not know if that is a feature or a fault.

She does not use it on other people. Something about it felt uncomfortable, reading the moods of the workers who leaned against her in the narrow terraces. A tool that knew too much, held too close to the skin. She keeps it for the gardens and the storms and the slow weather of the dome.
